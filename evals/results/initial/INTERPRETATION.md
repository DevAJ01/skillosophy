# Initial findings and next test

The judge scored the skill-assisted answer higher in 3 of 24 pairs and tied the other 21. It reported no material skill-assisted failure on these cases. The baseline averaged 9.875/10, so the rubric had very little room to distinguish answers.

The three differences were explicit universalization in the Kant case, distinguishing uses of “intelligence” in the Wittgenstein case, and an explicit stopping condition in the Cartesian-doubt regression. These are plausible additions, but they do not establish better real-world decisions. In particular, the Kant advantage partly reflects a method-specific criterion; a customer could reasonably find both answers equally useful.

The strongest supported conclusion is that the workflows loaded successfully and handled these simple tasks without an observed regression. A claim that the collection provides a meaningful overall performance improvement would be premature.

A harder transfer suite was designed by the baseline-only agent without seeing the skills. The skill text is frozen for that test. The transfer suite includes real constraints, competing criteria, uncertainty, and requested artifacts. Its results will be reported separately, including ties and failures. Neither suite is a random sample or a cross-model evaluation.
