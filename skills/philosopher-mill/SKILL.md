# Millian Consequence Analysis

Make the consequences of alternatives explicit without pretending welfare is always measurable on one reliable scale. This is a Mill-inspired utilitarian lens with modern decision-analysis tools, not a claim that Mill used these tools.

## Method

Specify alternatives, including a feasible status quo, who is affected, and the time horizon. Include people bearing indirect costs and effects outside the decision maker's objective metric. Separate preferences, welfare, and business outcomes; revenue or engagement is not a welfare measure by definition.

Map likely benefits and harms, their severity, duration, likelihood, and distribution. Consider differences in quality of experience as well as quantity. Include trust, autonomy, predictable rules, and incentives in the consequences; do not reduce the analysis to immediate totals.

Use numerical expected values only when inputs and units are defensible. Label estimated inputs, do not add incommensurable quantities without a justified conversion, and show qualitative comparisons when evidence is weak. Distinguish “unknown” from zero. Identify concentrated serious harms even when the net total appears positive.

Inspect uncertainty and sensitivity. Explain which estimate, weight, or time horizon could reverse the ranking. Assess alternatives that preserve benefits while reducing harm, and consider whether a general rule produces different outcomes from an isolated exception.

Recommend the best-supported feasible option with mitigation, an observation that would change the recommendation, and a proportionate review plan. Treat stated rights or consent constraints transparently; explain whether they are external constraints or justified within this consequence analysis instead of silently changing frameworks.

## Useful output

Provide a stakeholder comparison, uncertainty that matters, a recommendation with mitigation, and a reversal condition. Show calculations only where their assumptions are auditable.

## Worked application — original example

Request: “Add a default reminder every hour because clicks increase.”
Clicks alone omit interruption, fatigue, opting out, and loss of trust. Compare user-selected timing, a digest, and the current behavior. Evaluate completion of the user's goal and reported burden alongside engagement, with special attention to people disproportionately interrupted.

## Failure checks

Have you counted only the organization or the majority? Have you invented probabilities or common units? Can a plausible change in assumptions reverse your recommendation?

## Boundaries

Use this as a reasoning lens, not a simulation of the philosopher or proof that a conclusion is correct. Follow the user's requested deliverable and constraints. Give concise reasons, assumptions, and decision criteria rather than a private deliberation transcript. Do not invent quotations, sources, historical positions, motives, or numerical evidence. Distinguish facts supplied by the user from hypotheses and value judgments. Historical claims require a checked source when precision matters; contemporary applications below are our adaptations. A lens supplies no authority to take external actions.

Scale the method to the stakes. For a simple request, use only the move that changes the answer. If the method adds no useful distinction, say so and complete the task directly. End with a usable answer or next action, not philosophy vocabulary alone.
